My own terms

Brand Infrastructure

The machine-readable layer of a brand: explicit, parametrizable, for systems.

It complements brand identity, which addresses people, rather than replacing it. A brand needs both at once: identity for people, infrastructure for systems. Older uses of the expression exist in a different sense, for example for organizational brand processes.

Read the article → · Used since March 2026

Brand Specification

The brand in a form that can be applied rather than interpreted.

Positioning, language rules, facts, and boundaries, written for people and systems at once. A brand book describes a brand and leaves the reading to the reader. A specification states what holds, and can therefore be executed. That difference decides whether a brand appears in automated processes at all.

Used since March 2026 · Read the definition →

Machine Readable Brands

Brands whose identity, language, and design logic exist as a specification that people and systems can work with equally.

Third stage of a maturity model: being read, producing with AI, becoming applicable. A brand book describes a brand; a specification can be applied.

Read the definition → · Used since March 2026

Agentic Brand Readiness

The measurable maturity level of a brand toward AI systems: being found, described correctly, and recommended.

Stage one of the ladder, and the only one you can test yourself in ten minutes: three questions to ChatGPT or Perplexity, four points of evaluation.

Read the article → · Used since March 2026

Machine Consensus

The picture of a brand that AI systems assemble from every source they can find.

Not what a brand says about itself, but what systems synthesize from its website, trade press, reviews, and forums. For anyone asking an AI, that consensus is the brand. It cannot be controlled, but it can be influenced: through the sources that feed into it.

Used since March 2026 · Read the article →

Machine View

A brand as systems process it: source code, structure and markup instead of design.

What AI search, crawlers and browser agents receive from a page is not the designed but the marked-up: text, roles, names, structure. The demonstration of the same name sets both views side by side and shows what gets lost on the way. Since August 2026 also the title of the Maschinensicht column at The Business of Brand Management.

Introduced July 2026 · To the demonstration → · To the essay →

Execution Layer / Layer of Meaning

A brand's two layers: how it looks and sounds (execution), and who it is and what holds for it (meaning).

The execution layer corresponds to classic corporate design and has become partly machine-capable through design systems and component libraries. The layer of meaning corresponds to corporate identity and almost everywhere lives in sentences a human has to interpret. Toward machines it needs a sentence that would be unnecessary for a human audience: what is meant to apply must also exist as text.

Introduced August 2026 · To the article →

Agent Authority

The deliberately granted, bounded, and accounted-for mandate a brand gives its agents.

Granted means: decided rather than drifted into. Bounded means: defined what the agent may promise and where a human takes over. Accounted for means: there is an address that stands behind it. The expression comes from the law of agency and is carried into the brand context here.

Read the article → · Used since July 2026

Accountable Decision

The judgment about which option is right: justified, dated, versioned, with a named sender who stands behind it.

What remains of brand work when machines take over execution. An opinion is stated and left behind. An accountable decision carries consequences. Opinions are free, including from machines.

Read the article → · Used since July 2026

Consensus Taste

Taste that can be learned from many examples, and therefore reproduces the middle.

What many people consider good can be trained. When that middle comes out of a machine, we call it slop. The objection is not aimed at the machine but at the assumption that taste is an average. A decision that risks something is by definition not the middle.

Used since July 2026 · Read the article →

The Bet

The recommendation that commits: thesis, named failure signal, sender who bears the consequences.

What strategy work sells when analysis is available in minutes. Language models deliver plausibility, meaning the most likely consensus. A commitment is the opposite of that.

Read the article → · Bet is common strategy vocabulary; used in this sense since July 2026

Failure Signal

The named event at which a recommendation visibly fails.

Without a failure signal, a strategy paper is not finished; it is plausibility in a nice format. A failure signal is a sentence, not a model with two decimal places: how will we know in twelve months that this recommendation does not hold? The RAND Corporation calls such a signal a signpost.

Read the article → · Used since July 2026

Stateful

A brand carries its entire history with it. Every statement lands against everything said before.

A language model is stateless: each request starts from zero, with no memory of the last. Brands work the other way around. What you promise today is measured against what you promised three years ago. So it is not enough to tell a system how a brand sounds. It has to know what the brand has already said.

Used since March 2026 · No article yet

Identity File

The file that states who a brand is and what it acts on.

In software circles the name SOUL.md circulates for this. Identity file is the more precise expression: less pathos, clearer about the thing. It is not a document about the brand but the place where the brand exists for systems. What is not in it does not get applied.

Used since March 2026 · No article yet

Open Brand Repo

The brand specification as a public, versioned repository.

Identity, language, and facts readable for agents and verifiable for people, while the boundaries of what an agent may promise stay internal. Trust through verifiability instead of assertion. Every rule a documented decision, with date and sender.

Used since July 2026 · Read the article →

Second List

The short list of brand elements a brand deliberately withholds from the machine.

Every item needs two entries: an address that stands behind it, and proof that it actually works that way. Counterpart to the first list, which holds everything the machine may deliver — that one should be long and become cheap, because only the cheap surface makes the exception visibly valuable.

Used since August 2026 · Read the article →

Terms I use

Terms from the field that appear in my writing. Not coined by me, explained here.

GEO

Optimizing content for the answer a generative system gives, rather than for a ranking in a list of results.

Generative Engine Optimization. SEO gets you found; GEO gets you into the answer. Both remain work on discoverability. Machine Readable Brands start one step earlier: with the question of whether anything applicable exists that a system can work with.

Used in Machine Readable Brands and Brand Infrastructure

Answer Engine / AEO

A system that answers a question directly instead of linking to results. AEO is the practice of optimizing for it.

The difference from a search engine is the missing list: there is one answer, not ten options. If you are not in it, you are not there. In practice AEO overlaps heavily with GEO; the split is more the market's vocabulary than a real distinction.

Used in Agentic Brand Readiness

Domain Authority

A metric from SEO tools estimating the strength of a domain from its link profile.

It comes from tool vendors, not from search engines. For visibility in AI answers it carries little: across a survey of 75,000 brands, third-party mentions correlate at 0.66 to 0.74, domain authority at 0.27 to 0.33. What others say about a brand weighs more than what the brand writes about itself.

Citation

The named mention of a source in an AI system’s answer, usually with a link.

The currency of AI search: whoever is cited appears. A visit it is not. When an AI summary appears, users click a classic result on 8 percent of visits, and on 15 percent without one. And it is volatile: Reddit’s share of the sources ChatGPT cites fell from 3.83 to 0.52 percent within a few days.

Evidence on click-through → · Evidence on volatility →

llms.txt

A text file summarizing a website's most important content for language models.

Proposed in 2024 and heavily promoted since. For coding and browser agents that are handed the file directly, it is useful. As a visibility lever it is, by measurement, ineffective: a server-log analysis across 137,210 domains found that 97 percent of all llms.txt files received no request at all in May 2026. This site has one, for the first reason, not the second.

Used in Brand Infrastructure

robots.txt

Text file in the root directory telling crawlers which areas they may retrieve.

Older and more effective than llms.txt, and a request rather than a barrier. For AI crawlers it is today the only widely used means of control, and it steers through tokens that each vendor assigns and can change at any time. No common standard for this exists.

Technical term · Evidence on the state of standardization →

Schema.org / JSON-LD

A shared vocabulary for marking up who or what a website is. JSON-LD is the usual format for it.

The least exciting and most effective part of brand infrastructure. A person can see that a page holds a person, a product, or a price. A system has to be told. Without that markup, a statement stays prose a system has to interpret instead of adopt.

Used in Brand Infrastructure and Agentic Brand Readiness

Structured Data

Statements on a page that do not merely sit there but are marked up as what they are.

The umbrella term for Schema.org and JSON-LD. Google records in its own guidance that AI search needs no special markup; for rich results in classic search the markup remains useful. That both hold at once is why the question of whether markup helps is so often answered wrongly.

Used in Brand Infrastructure · Evidence →

Special Markup

Additional markup intended specifically for AI systems.

Google declares it expressly unnecessary in its own guidance, together with llms.txt and custom chunking. That is the inconvenient answer for a whole market of tools. What helps is less spectacular: reachable pages, and clean markup of what is there anyway.

Evidence →

Accessibility Tree

The structure the browser computes from every page: all elements with role and name, no layout.

Built for screen readers, and according to Google's developer documentation now the primary interface for browser agents. An element without an accessible name does not exist in this view, however prominently it is designed. Accessibility is no longer a niche; it is the ticket into the machine view.

Term from accessibility · Used in The Gap That Doesn't Respond and the Machine View demonstration

ARIA Landmark

Markup telling a system which part of a page is main content, navigation or footer.

It answers where the content begins and the navigation ends. Without it a program has to infer that from the design, and design is what it does not see. In our own survey, 52 of 135 home pages of German listed companies carry no main-content landmark.

Term from accessibility · Evidence →

Agentic Browsing

An audit category in Google's Lighthouse measuring how well agents can read and operate a website.

Since Lighthouse 13.3.0 of 7 May 2026 an experimental category in the default configuration, usable from Chrome 150. The audit tool itself is built into Chrome. Agent readability turns from claim into score. For context: a good score proves readability, not visibility, and Google itself states that AI search needs no special files.

Term by Google (Lighthouse), 2026 · Used in The Gap That Doesn't Respond · Evidence

Browser Agent

Software operating websites on a user's behalf: comparing, booking, buying instead of just reading.

Distinct from two neighbors: a crawler collects content for an index, a chatbot answers from what it has learned. A browser agent acts, in real time and with consequences. It reads pages through their structure, the accessibility tree, and takes literally what it finds there. For brands it is the new audience: the reader that fills in nothing.

Established term · Used in The Gap That Doesn't Respond and Agent Authority

Bot Defence

Technology that detects and refuses automated access.

In our own survey, 12 of 160 home pages refuse an automated request, mostly through Akamai or Cloudflare. The point of it: the defence recognises the tool, not the intent. An agent sent by a customer to check availability is turned away just like a scraper.

Used in Machine View · Evidence →

User Agent

The string with which a program identifies itself when retrieving a page.

It is self-declared and can be set freely. Bot defence nonetheless rests largely on it. For brands that means two things: steering crawlers means steering through a statement anyone can forge, and locking out an agent may mean locking out a customer.

Technical term · Used in Machine View

Canonical Link

The machine-readable commitment declaring which version of a piece of content is the original.

A reference in a page's source code (rel=canonical) stating where its original lives. Search engines and AI systems then credit the original address; duplicates do no harm. This site applies it itself: the guest essay "The Gap That Doesn't Respond" lives here in the archive and points via canonical to its first publication at The Business of Brand Management.

Web standard · Applied in The Gap That Doesn't Respond

Design Token

The smallest machine-readable design decision: a color, a spacing, a typeface as a named value.

Instead of "blue as in the PDF", a token holds a named, versionable value tools can apply directly. Since 28 October 2025 a stable specification exists, published by the Design Tokens Community Group at the W3C. It is explicitly not a W3C Standard and not on the W3C Standards Track. Tokens prove that a brand's execution layer can become machine-capable. For the layer of meaning, the counterpart is still missing.

Established term, stable specification since 2025 · Used in Open Brand Repo

Model Context Protocol

An open standard through which AI systems reach external tools and data sources.

Introduced by Anthropic in November 2024. OpenAI adopted it in March 2025, other providers followed the same year. Since 9 December 2025 stewardship has rested with the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation. For brands the technology matters less than the consequence: there is now a standard route by which an agent reaches brand data. Anyone with nothing available along that route does not exist for agents.

Used in Brand Infrastructure

AI Act

The European Union regulation on artificial intelligence.

For brands what counts is above all Article 50: chatbots must identify themselves, and AI-generated content must carry machine-readable marking. The duty does not always fall on whom it appears to. The marking is owed by the provider of the generating system, not by the brand using a third-party model. The deadlines have been moved twice since 2024.

Machine Customers

Systems that buy on their own or prepare purchasing decisions.

Coined by Gartner. In a survey by the firm, chief executives estimate that by 2030, 15 to 20 percent of their own company's revenue will come from machines as customers. That is a self-assessment by those surveyed, not a house forecast from Gartner. What matters is not the number but the shift in addressee: brand communication then speaks to a customer that sees no images.

Used in Brand Infrastructure · Evidence

Preprint

A scientific paper that is published but not yet peer reviewed.

That makes it citable and unchecked at once. On the evidence page this stands as a limit on every entry resting on a preprint. The difference is not a formality: peer review is where specialists get to attack the method before a figure goes into circulation.

A limit on several entries

Agentic Commerce

Commerce in which an agent on at least one side compares, negotiates, or buys.

An actual purchase by an agent is currently the rarest case. In May 2026 Forrester described true autonomy as rare: very few consumers let an agent complete a task or a purchase without direct oversight, and fewer still do so regularly. More common is the step before it: agents that compare, filter, and recommend. None of the sources checked puts a revenue figure on it. The recommendation decides earlier than the transaction.

Used in Agent Authority and Brand Infrastructure · Evidence: checkout rollback and purchase decision

Universal Commerce Protocol

Open standard for purchasing through agents, carried by a body of several vendors.

The counterpart to the Model Context Protocol on the commerce side. The body is staffed and the announcements are numerous; to this day no source gives a figure for adoption, Google included. A seat on a standards body does not establish adoption.
